NSSF Tier I vs Tier II in Kenya: Simple Breakdown

Know exactly what gets deducted and why your NSSF amount changes with salary.

NSSF in Kenya is no longer a flat deduction. Under the NSSF Act 2013, contribution is based on salary and split into two parts: Tier I and Tier II. This design helps standardise pension contributions while placing limits on the maximum monthly deduction. If you've ever wondered why your NSSF deduction changed when you got a raise, or why two colleagues with different salaries both see the same NSSF figure, this two-tier structure is the reason.

Rates updated: NSSF entered Phase 4 of its five-phase rollout on 1 February 2026, raising both tier thresholds. The figures below reflect the current Phase 4 rates.

Tier I: First KES 9,000

Tier I covers the first KES 9,000 of pensionable earnings. The employee contributes 6% of that amount and the employer contributes the same. So the employee Tier I deduction is capped at KES 540 per month. Every employee earning at least KES 9,000 pays exactly this amount for Tier I — it doesn't matter whether your salary is KES 10,000 or KES 500,000, the Tier I portion is fixed once you clear that first threshold.

Tier II: Next KES 99,000

Tier II applies to salary between KES 9,001 and KES 108,000. The employee rate remains 6%, matched by the employer. If your salary is above KES 108,000, Tier II is still capped at KES 99,000 of earnings — meaning NSSF contributions stop growing entirely once your pensionable earnings pass KES 108,000, regardless of how much more you earn.

Maximum employee NSSF deduction

When salary reaches KES 108,000 or more, the employee deduction reaches its monthly ceiling:

  • Tier I: KES 540
  • Tier II: KES 5,940
  • Total employee NSSF: KES 6,480

The employer matches this exactly, contributing another KES 6,480, so the total monthly contribution going into your NSSF account at this earnings level is KES 12,960 — even though only KES 6,480 shows up as a deduction on your payslip.

Quick examples

  • KES 30,000 salary: Tier I (540) + Tier II on 21,000 (1,260) = KES 1,800.
  • KES 50,000 salary: Tier I (540) + Tier II on 41,000 (2,460) = KES 3,000.
  • KES 100,000 salary: Tier I (540) + Tier II on 91,000 (5,460) = KES 6,000 — not yet at the cap.
  • KES 108,000 salary or above: capped at KES 6,480.

Notice that the KES 30,000 and KES 50,000 totals happen to match what they would have been under the pre-February-2026 rules too — that's not an error, it's because both salaries fall well under either version of the upper limit, so only the tier split changes, not the total. It's the higher salary bands, like KES 100,000, where the February 2026 increase actually raises what you pay: that salary used to be fully capped at KES 4,320, and now sits below the (higher) new cap, paying KES 6,000 instead.

Where does the money actually go?

NSSF is a mandatory retirement savings scheme, not a tax — the contributions (both yours and your employer's) are credited to your individual NSSF account and invested on your behalf. Unlike PAYE, which funds general government spending, NSSF money remains attributable to you personally and is paid out as a pension benefit, typically when you reach retirement age, become permanently incapacitated, or in specific circumstances defined under the Act. This is why it's worth checking your NSSF statement periodically — the contributions your employer has deducted from you should actually be remitted and reflected in your account.

The five-phase rollout

Before the NSSF Act 2013 came into full effect, contributions were a flat KES 200 per employee per month — a token amount that provided very little in retirement savings. The tiered, percentage-based system replaced this specifically to build a more meaningful pension pot over a working career, scaling contributions with actual earnings rather than a fixed nominal figure. Rather than jumping straight to the final rates, the Act phases the increase in over five annual steps. Phase 4 took effect on 1 February 2026, raising the Tier I limit from KES 8,000 to KES 9,000 and the Tier II limit from KES 72,000 to KES 108,000 — the contribution rate itself stayed at 6% throughout; only the earnings bands it applies to have grown. A further increase (Phase 5) is expected to follow in a future year as the rollout completes.

Why this matters for payroll

NSSF is one of the key figures used before PAYE is computed. Wrong NSSF means wrong PAYE and wrong net salary. For employers, this creates compliance risk, including potential penalties for under-remittance. For employees, it creates trust issues when payslips differ from expected figures — and since NSSF directly reduces PAYE taxable pay, an error here cascades into every other deduction on the payslip.
